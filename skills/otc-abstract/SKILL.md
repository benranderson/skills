# Writing an OTC Abstract

## Overview

OTC abstracts are screened by a program committee against fixed technical criteria. A strong abstract states plainly what was done, how, what was found, and why it is new - in 225-450 words. This skill captures the structure, criteria, and constraints so a draft is accepted, not declined.

**REQUIRED BACKGROUND:** Use signs-of-ai-writing while drafting. Committee reviewers reject vague, promotional, and generic prose. Keep every sentence specific and factual.

## When to use

- Drafting a new abstract for any OTC conference
- Restructuring existing text to fit OTC's required elements
- Reviewing a draft before submission against selection criteria

## Required elements

Include all five, in order. Reviewers look for each explicitly. Per-section word budgets below come from OTC's sample abstract and total the 225-450 word range.

| Element | Words | What to write |
|---------|-------|---------------|
| **Title** | - | Short, informative, familiar terms. Avoid "new" and "improved". |
| **Objectives / Scope** | 25-75 | The objectives and scope of the proposed paper. No literature overview. |
| **Methods, Procedures, Process** | 75-100 | The overall approach. Answer *what* and *how*. Give enough detail for a reader to replicate results. |
| **Results, Observations, Conclusions** | 100-200 | Actual findings, quantified. Lead with the single most significant new finding, then why it matters. Do not repeat results in the conclusion. |
| **Novel / Additive Information** | 25-75 | What new or additive knowledge this adds, and its benefit to a practising engineer. |

## Selection criteria

The committee accepts abstracts that:

- Contribute technically sound knowledge in a specific area of petroleum technology
- Present new/novel knowledge or experience not previously published
- Are **not commercial** - no company, product, service, or trade names

Failing any of these gets an abstract declined regardless of quality.

## Constraints

- **Length:** 225-450 words. Stay factual and you will not feel constrained.
- **Language:** English only.
- **One conference at a time:** dual submission is prohibited.
- **No paper, no podium:** acceptance obligates you to submit a full manuscript (min. 3,000 words) by the deadline, or you cannot present.
- **Finality:** an abstract cannot be edited after submission - only minor title/author changes at the manuscript stage. Proofread before submitting.
- **Figures optional:** one figure, table, or image may be uploaded during submission.
- **Deadline:** the system closes at 11:59 p.m. US Central Time on the stated date. No automated confirmation email is sent - save the summary page.

## Drafting tips

- Answer "what" and "how" in the methods; do not open with a literature survey.
- Quantify findings ("contact forces two to three times higher than static models") rather than asserting significance.
- State specific innovations; avoid claiming something is "groundbreaking".
- Keep it succinct, relevant, and convincing.

## Worked example

OTC's own sample abstract, "Dynamic Model for Stiff String Torque and Drag", shows the target register - specific, quantified, non-promotional:

- **Objectives/Scope:** "A stiff string torque and drag model is presented that uses steady state dynamic equilibrium of the drillstring as its basis for calculations. Results are compared to previously published T&D models that are based on static equilibrium."
- **Methods:** names the model type (3D transient dynamic), the physics it accounts for (bending/torsional stiffness, contact forces, friction), and notes complete governing equations are provided "to permit readers to replicate all results".
- **Results:** compares against named model classes, presents four field case studies with concrete dogleg severities, then leads with the key finding - "local contact forces and bending moments can be two to three times higher than static models" - and lists why it matters (casing wear, downhole tool operation, fatigue).
- **Novelty:** one sentence - solving T&D for the entire drillstring "in reasonable time using standard engineering computers".

Note how each section carries specifics (numbers, named methods, field cases) and none names a commercial product.

## Common mistakes

- **Commercial tone** - naming a company or product. Use generic descriptions.
- **Padding with significance claims** - "plays a pivotal role", "underscores the importance". Cut them (see signs-of-ai-writing).
- **Restating results in the conclusion** - the conclusion should add interpretation, not repeat.
- **Skipping the novelty statement** - reviewers score this explicitly; make it unmistakable.
- **Vague objectives** buried under background - lead with the objective.
- **Unquantified results** - the sample succeeds because findings are numeric; "significantly higher" is weaker than "two to three times higher".
