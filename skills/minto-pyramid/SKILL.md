# Structuring Business Communication with the Minto Pyramid

## Overview

The Minto Pyramid Principle: **state the answer first, then support it with a pyramid of grouped, summarised ideas.** Readers absorb a message fastest when they get the governing thought up front and every supporting idea sits in a labelled group beneath it.

The failure this fixes: writers structure a message in the order they *discovered* it - options they weighed, the chronology of the work - so the reader has to wade through detail before learning the point. Restructure so the reader gets the answer immediately and the reasoning falls into place beneath it.

**REQUIRED BACKGROUND:** Pair with signs-of-ai-writing when drafting the prose. Pyramid fixes *structure*; signs-of-ai-writing keeps the *sentences* specific and non-promotional.

## When to use

- Writing a recommendation, memo, email, report, or proposal
- Building a slide deck or presentation narrative
- Any status update or briefing to leadership
- Reviewing a draft where the reader has to hunt for the point
- Turning raw notes or analysis into a clear message

**When not to use:** pure reference material (API docs, tables), creative writing, or a message with genuinely one idea and no supporting structure.

## The one test: can the reader stop after the first paragraph?

If a busy reader read only your opening and nothing else, would they know your answer and what you want them to do? If not, the pyramid is upside down. Fix that before anything else.

## Step 1 - Frame the reader's question with SCQA

Never open cold with your answer. Set up the *question in the reader's mind* first, then answer it. Use SCQA - a 3-4 sentence introduction:

| Element | What it does | Example |
|---------|--------------|---------|
| **Situation** | A fact the reader already accepts. Non-controversial. | "Support tickets are up 40% this quarter." |
| **Complication** | What changed or went wrong - creates tension. | "Churn is starting to rise as a result." |
| **Question** | The question the Complication provokes (often implicit). | *"What should we do about it?"* |
| **Answer** | Your governing thought. The top of the pyramid. | "Deploy the AI chatbot now as our first move." |

The Answer is your headline. Everything else in the message supports it.

## Step 2 - The three rules of a valid pyramid

Every pyramid must obey all three. Break one and the logic leaks.

1. **Ideas at each level summarise the ideas grouped below them.** A parent box is the one-sentence takeaway of its children - not a topic label like "Costs".
2. **Ideas in each group are the same kind of idea (MECE).** Mutually Exclusive (no overlap) and Collectively Exhaustive (nothing missing). Reasons group with reasons, steps with steps.
3. **Ideas in each group are logically ordered** (see Step 4).

## Step 3 - Connect the levels with Q&A and choose your logic

**Vertical (between levels): question-and-answer.** Each box should raise a single question in the reader's mind that the group directly below answers. If a supporting point doesn't answer the question its parent provokes, it's in the wrong place.

**Horizontal (within a group): inductive or deductive.**

- **Inductive** - a set of the same type of idea, labelled with a plural noun. "Three reasons", "four risks", "two steps". Most business writing is inductive and easier to read. The group needs a single summarising sentence, not just a category.
- **Deductive** - a chain: premise → premise → therefore. "Retention is the priority. Outsourcing hurt retention at a peer. Therefore rule out outsourcing." Powerful but tiring if stacked; use sparingly and prefer inductive at the top level.

## Step 4 - Order ideas within each group

Pick the ordering that matches the group's logic, then apply it consistently:

| Order | Use when the group is... | Ordered by |
|-------|--------------------------|------------|
| **Deductive** | An argument chain | Premise, then conclusion |
| **Chronological / time** | Steps in a process or plan | First to last |
| **Structural** | Parts of a whole (org, geography, system) | The way the whole divides |
| **Comparative / degree** | Ranked items | Most important first |

For a recommendation to a decision-maker, default to **most important first** - lead with the argument that most moves their decision.

## Step 5 - Build it (top-down preferred)

**Top-down** (when you know the answer):
1. Write the box at the top - your answer.
2. Ask: what question does this raise for the reader?
3. Answer it with a MECE group of supporting points.
4. Recurse until each point is self-evident or backed by data.

**Bottom-up** (when the answer is still fuzzy):
1. List every point, fact, and idea you have.
2. Group the ones that are the same kind of idea.
3. Summarise each group in one sentence - that sentence becomes the parent.
4. The single sentence that summarises all groups is your answer.

## Applying it to formats

- **Email / memo:** Subject line = the answer or the action needed, not the topic. First paragraph = SCQA. Body = the supporting group, one point per paragraph or bullet, ordered by importance. Close with the specific next step.
- **Status update:** Lead with overall verdict + any decision you need *now*. Then group: on track / at risk / needs decision. Never open with a chronology of what happened.
- **Slide deck:** The horizontal flow of slide *titles*, read alone, should tell the whole story top-down (action titles, not topic titles). Each slide's body supports its title.
- **Presentation:** Say the answer in the first 60 seconds. The audience listens better once they know where you're headed.

## Worked example - recommendation email

**Before** (answer buried - reader learns the recommendation only in paragraph 3, after all three options):

> Subject: Recommendation to address support backlog
>
> Our backlog is up 40%... We weighed three options: hire 2 agents ($180k/yr, 3 months)... outsource ($120k/yr, but a peer saw satisfaction drop 15%)... an AI chatbot ($40k/yr, live in 3 weeks, ~30% deflection). I recommend we start with the chatbot...

**After** (pyramid - SCQA intro, answer first, three reasons ordered by what the VP cares about):

> **Subject: Recommend deploying an AI chatbot now to cut the support backlog**
>
> Support tickets are up 40% this quarter and churn is starting to rise *(Situation + Complication)*. Of the three options we evaluated, I recommend we deploy the AI chatbot as our first move *(Answer)*. Three reasons:
>
> - **It protects retention, which is the priority.** Outsourcing cut satisfaction 15% at a peer; the chatbot carries no such risk.
> - **It's the fastest relief.** Live in 3 weeks vs. 3 months to ramp new hires, and the vendor pilot deflected ~30% of tickets.
> - **It's the cheapest and least committal.** $40k/yr vs. $180k for hiring - and it buys time to decide whether we still need headcount.
>
> **Next step:** a one-year contract with a 60-day check-in on deflection and satisfaction. If the numbers don't hold, we revisit hiring.

The reader can stop after sentence two and still know the answer. The three reasons are one MECE group (all "why the chatbot"), ordered most-important-first for this reader.

## Common mistakes

- **Answer buried in the middle** - the classic. Move the recommendation to the top.
- **Topic labels instead of takeaways** - "Costs" and "Risks" are categories, not ideas. A parent box must be a full sentence you could defend: "The chatbot is the lowest-cost option."
- **Chronological status updates** - leading with "First we did X, then Y" forces the reader to reconstruct the point. Lead with the verdict.
- **Non-MECE groups** - overlapping or gappy support. Check every group for both overlap and omissions.
- **Grouping different kinds of idea together** - a reason next to a step next to a risk. Split them into separate groups.
- **Skipping SCQA** - opening cold with the answer, with no Situation/Complication to make the reader care about the question.
- **Deductive chains stacked deep** - exhausting to read. Prefer inductive groups at the top; reserve deduction for a single tight argument.
- **Too many top-level points** - if a group has more than about five items, they probably subdivide into two or three real groups.
